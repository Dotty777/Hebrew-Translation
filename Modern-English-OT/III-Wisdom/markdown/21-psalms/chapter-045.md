## Text

**45:1** For the music director, of the sons of Korah, according to Alamoth; a song.

**45:2** God is a refuge and strength for us, a help in distresses, abundantly found.

**45:3** Therefore we will not be afraid, even when the earth changes and when mountains totter into the heart of the seas,

**45:4** though its waters roar and seethe, though mountains quake at its swelling. Selah.

**45:5** A river—its streams gladden the city of God, the holy place of the dwellings of the Most High.

**45:6** God is in her midst; she will not totter; God will help her at the break of morning.

**45:7** Nations roared, kingdoms tottered; He gave forth His voice, the earth melted.

**45:8** The LORD of hosts is with us; a high fortress for us is the God of Jacob. Selah.

**45:9** Come, gaze upon the deeds of the LORD, who has made desolations on the earth.

**45:10** He causes wars to cease as far as the end of the earth; He shatters the bow and cuts the spear apart; He burns wagons in the fire.

**45:11** "Desist, and know that I am God; I will be lifted high among the nations, I will be lifted high on the earth."

**45:12** The LORD of hosts is with us; a high fortress for us is the God of Jacob. Selah.

## Translator Notes

- **45:1** — Alamoth literally means young women or maidens, a musical term found also in 1 Chronicles 15:20 that likely indicates a high vocal register or soprano accompaniment.
- **45:2** — Literally a help in distresses He is exceedingly found; the masculine Niphal form nimtsa agrees with God rather than the feminine noun help, stressing that God proves abundantly present in trouble.
- **45:3** — The Hiphil infinitive construct hamir, from the root mwr meaning to change or exchange, is used intransitively here for the earth undergoing upheaval or giving way.
- **45:10** — Hebrew agalot literally denotes wagons or war-carts, distinct from the usual word for chariots; the Septuagint translates the term as shields.
- **45:11** — Desist translates the Hiphil imperative harpu, literally let drop or slacken one's hands. The repeated verb arum is Qal, expressing God's own rising high in exaltation.
