## Text

**6:1** Where did your beloved go, most beautiful among women? Where did your beloved turn, so we may seek him with you?

**6:2** My beloved went down to his garden, to the beds of spice, to graze in the gardens and to pick lilies.

**6:3** I belong to my beloved, and my beloved belongs to me, he who grazes among the lilies.

**6:4** You are beautiful, my companion, like Tirzah, lovely like Jerusalem, awe-inspiring as armies with banners.

**6:5** Turn your eyes away from me, for they have overpowered me. Your hair is like a flock of goats gliding down from Gilead.

**6:6** Your teeth are like a flock of ewes coming up from the washing, all of them bearing twins, with not a bereaved one among them.

**6:7** Like a slice of pomegranate is your cheek behind your veil.

**6:8** Sixty queens there are, and eighty concubines, and young women without number.

**6:9** One alone is she, my dove, my flawless one; one alone is she to her mother, pure is she to the one who bore her. Daughters saw her and pronounced her blessed; queens and concubines, and they praised her.

**6:10** Who is this looking out like the dawn, beautiful as the moon, pure as the sun, awe-inspiring as armies with banners?

**6:11** I went down to the nut garden to look at the fresh shoots of the valley, to see if the vine had budded, if the pomegranates had bloomed.

**6:12** Before I knew it, my soul placed me in the chariots of my noble people.

## Translator Notes

- **6:4** — Hebrew nidgalot, rendered armies with banners and repeated in 6:10, is a Niphal feminine plural participle from degel, banner, referring to bannered hosts, though some connect it to an Akkadian cognate meaning splendid sights or celestial bodies.
- **6:5** — Hebrew hirhibuni, they have overpowered me, is the Hiphil perfect of rahab, conveying overwhelming or unsettling force; unlike 4:1, the Masoretic Text here reads from Gilead without Mount.
- **6:12** — This verse is notoriously difficult, literally reading I did not know, my soul placed me the chariots of my noble people; the two-word Masoretic phrase ammi nadiv is also sometimes understood as a personal name, Amminadib, or as my kinsman, a prince.
